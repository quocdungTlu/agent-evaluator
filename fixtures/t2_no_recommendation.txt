Bayesian statistics reframes probability as a degree of belief rather than a long-run frequency, which is the key mental shift for anyone coming from classical hypothesis testing. Instead of asking "how likely is this data given the null hypothesis," you ask "how should my belief about the parameter update given this data," using Bayes' rule to combine a prior distribution with a likelihood into a posterior.

For a working data scientist, the most useful shift is usually in how you think about uncertainty: instead of a single p-value, you get a full posterior distribution over the parameter, which naturally gives you credible intervals and lets you reason about the probability of specific hypotheses directly — something a frequentist confidence interval was never designed to do. Hierarchical models are where this really pays off, since partial pooling across groups often gives better estimates than either fully separate or fully pooled models.

The main practical costs are computational: exact posteriors are rarely available in closed form outside of conjugate priors, so most real work leans on MCMC sampling (via tools like Stan or PyMC) or variational approximations, both of which require some judgment about convergence diagnostics that frequentist workflows don't ask for.
